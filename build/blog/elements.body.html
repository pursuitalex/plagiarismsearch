A second-level heading opens a section of the article

The paragraph under a heading sits close to it, and the heading sits well clear of what came before: the space above a heading is larger than the space below it, so a heading always reads as belonging to the text it introduces.

A third-level heading divides a section

It is smaller and lighter than the heading above it, and larger and heavier than the text under it.

A fourth-level heading names a point inside a subsection

It is set at the size of body text, in bold: enough to be found when scanning, not enough to compete with the headings above it.

A fifth-level heading is rarely needed

When an old article uses one, it stays a heading and stays quieter than the fourth level.

A sixth-level heading reads as a label

It is set small, in capitals, and marks the smallest division an article can have.

A very long second-level heading, of the kind an archive is full of, that runs past the end of one line on a desktop and across four or five on a phone without breaking the rhythm of the page

A third-level heading placed directly under a second-level heading, with nothing between them

Two headings in a row keep the gap of the upper one: the lower heading does not add its own space above, so the pair reads as a title and its subtitle.
